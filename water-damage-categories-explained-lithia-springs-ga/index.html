What the three categories mean

Under the ANSI/IICRC S500 standard, water damage is sorted by the contamination level of the source. Category 1 comes from a sanitary source and poses no substantial risk at the start. Category 2 carries significant contamination and could cause discomfort or illness if people contact or swallow it. Category 3 is grossly contaminated and may carry harmful agents.

The label is about the water, not about how much of it there is. A single cup of sewage is still Category 3, while a flooded room from a clean supply line is still Category 1 when it starts.

This post stays on one question: which category is this water, and why does that change everything that follows?

Category 1: water that starts clean

A pinhole in a copper line, a cracked fill valve or a tub left running is usually Category 1. The water is safe at the moment it spills.

That does not mean it stays safe. Clean water becomes Category 2 as it soaks into materials, picks up dirt, and sits. The S500 standard recognizes that time and temperature change the category, so a clean leak that sat in a warm house for a couple of days may need to be treated as a higher category.

Speed is the main lever. Clean water caught early lets crews dry carpet and many other materials in place instead of hauling them to the curb.

Category 2: gray water with some contamination

Gray water comes from sources such as a washing machine discharge or a dishwasher, where soap, food debris and bacteria are present. A sump pump that fails and lets groundwater into a basement can also land here.

Porous materials in contact with gray water, especially carpet pad and the lower section of drywall, are generally removed. The reason is that you cannot reliably clean a sponge-like material that holds contaminated water deep inside.

Hard surfaces and structural wood are usually cleaned and treated, then dried. The crew documents what was done so the file is clear later.

Category 3 is a safety issue first

Keep people and pets out of sewage or floodwater. Do not walk through it, do not run a household vacuum on it, and do not try to wipe it up with a mop and bucket.

If water is near outlets, appliances or a panel, stay away. Cut power only if the panel can be reached while standing on a dry surface, and call the utility or 911 for any electrical or gas risk before calling the restoration line.

If you must step in briefly, wear gloves, boots and eye protection, then wash thoroughly afterward. Health questions belong to a physician, not to a restoration company.

Why floodwater is always Category 3

Rising water from a creek, river or storm drain crosses lawns, roads, septic fields and storage areas before it reaches a house. Under S500 it is treated as Category 3 regardless of how clear it looks.

That has real consequences for what stays and goes. Wet carpet, pad, insulation and drywall in contact with floodwater are normally removed, and hard surfaces are cleaned and disinfected before drying.

Insurance matters here. Homeowners policies usually exclude flood damage, and flood coverage is a separate policy. Check your policy and ask your insurer before you assume anything is covered.

Floodwater and Category 3 in the Lithia Springs area

The National Weather Service reports that the Sweetwater Creek basin reached the 500-year flood level in September 2009, and the USGS measured the creek at the I-20 bridge in Lithia Springs during that event. Douglas County also describes Woodrow Wilson Park on Mt. Vernon Road as lying in the Sweetwater Creek 100-year floodplain.

The practical tie-back is that creek-driven water in this area is Category 3 water. If you live near the creek corridor, look up your address on the FEMA flood map and ask your insurer whether a separate flood policy applies.

The Georgia Office of Commissioner of Insurance states that a homeowners policy does not cover flood, and that flood insurance is available only if the county participates in the national program. Ask the county if your community participates, because that decides whether coverage can be bought.

Classes are different from categories

People mix up the two words. Category describes contamination. Class describes how much evaporation work the room needs, based on porous materials and wet surface area.

Category answers: how dirty?

It guides protective gear, cleaning, and what must be removed. The question is whether the water carried contaminants at the source or picked them up along the way.

Class answers: how hard to dry?

Class 1 has the least wet material and Class 4 involves deeply held water in dense materials. The class decides how much equipment and time the drying takes.

A job has both labels

A clean supply line in a hardwood room could be Category 1, Class 3. A creek flood in a carpeted basement could be Category 3 with a different class.

Labels can change

If clean water sits, a crew may reclassify it as the water contacts soil, debris or materials. Early response keeps the category lower.

How a crew decides which category you have

The assessment starts with the source. A technician asks where the water came from, how long it has been there, and what it crossed on the way. They look for odor, discoloration, debris and signs of backed-up drain water.

Moisture meters and a thermal imaging camera map where the water went, and a visual check tells them whether the water has sat in place long enough to degrade. The answer shapes the plan: dry in place, remove the porous parts, or contain the area.

You can help by telling the crew everything you know, including a toilet that ran slow before the overflow. Details like that can change a category.

Why the category affects the bill

Category 3 jobs involve more labor, more protective equipment, more disposal of removed materials and often more rebuild work. A Category 1 job may dry in place with no demolition at all.

That is the reason a repair estimate over the phone is only a guess. The category, the class and the materials all feed into the final scope. A real number needs someone to look at the damage.

Common Questions

Can I clean up Category 2 or 3 water myself?

Small, clean spills yes. Gray or black water is a different story, since it can carry bacteria and the porous materials it touched need to be removed properly. Keep people and pets out, and call a professional crew.

Does clean water ever become Category 3?

Yes. The S500 standard recognizes that water can degrade over time and with warmth, so a clean leak left for days may be handled as a higher category. Acting quickly keeps more material savable.

Is rainwater through a roof clean water?

It often starts as Category 1, but it picks up dust, insulation and roofing debris as it travels through an attic and down a ceiling. Crews assess it on site and treat it according to what it has crossed.

Can carpet be saved after Category 2 water?

Sometimes. The pad is usually removed and the carpet may be cleaned and dried in place if the contact was short. A crew checks the readings and the condition before recommending replacement.

Why does the Lithia Springs creek corridor matter for water categories?

Rising water from Sweetwater Creek is floodwater, and floodwater is Category 3 under S500. NWS records show the basin reached the 500-year level in September 2009, so check the FEMA flood map for your address and ask your insurer about separate flood coverage.

Will my homeowners policy cover Category 3 damage?

It depends on the source. Sewer backup and flood are often excluded unless you added coverage, while sudden accidental damage from a plumbing failure is often covered. Check your policy and ask your insurer.
